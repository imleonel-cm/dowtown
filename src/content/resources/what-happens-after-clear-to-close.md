Your lender says the words you’ve been waiting for:

> “You’re clear to close.”

It sounds like the transaction is finished.

Not quite.

Clear to close is a major milestone, but there are still a few important steps between loan approval and actually completing your home purchase. Documents still need to be finalized and signed. Funds need to be ready. And in California, the required documents still need to be recorded before the transaction is complete.

So what actually happens next?

## Quick Answer: What Happens After Clear to Close?

After your loan is clear to close, you’ll typically review your final loan details, sign closing documents, make sure any required funds are available, wait for the lender to fund the loan, and then complete recording.

In California, recording is an important final step in completing the real estate transaction.

That means **clear to close doesn’t mean closed**.

It means you’re getting close.

## First, What Does “Clear to Close” Actually Mean?

“Clear to close,” often shortened to **CTC**, is a term used during the mortgage process.

It generally means the lender has completed underwriting and approved the loan to move forward toward closing. In other words, the major loan conditions have been satisfied and the file can move into the final stages.

That’s good news.

But loan approval is only one part of the closing process.

Escrow, the lender, the buyer, the seller, title and other parties may still have final items to complete before the transaction can officially close.

That distinction is where buyers sometimes get confused.

You can be clear to close and still not be ready to pick up the keys.

## Step 1: Review Your Final Loan Details

Before closing, borrowers using most traditional mortgage loans receive a **Closing Disclosure**.

This document shows the final details of the mortgage, including the loan amount, interest rate, estimated monthly payment and closing costs.

Federal rules generally require the lender to provide the Closing Disclosure at least **three business days before closing**, giving the borrower time to review the information and raise questions before signing.

The [CFPB explains the three-business-day requirement and Closing Disclosures](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/when-do-i-get-a-closing-disclosure-en-179/).

This isn’t a document to scroll through quickly and forget.

Look at the numbers. Does the loan amount look right? Is the interest rate what you expected? Do the closing costs make sense?

If something looks different from what you were expecting, ask about it before closing.

The closer you get to the finish line, the better it is to catch a question early rather than during the final signing.

## Step 2: Closing Documents Are Prepared and Signed

Once the loan and escrow are ready for the next stage, there are documents that need to be reviewed and signed.

For a financed purchase, those documents can include the promissory note, deed of trust and other loan and transaction documents. The exact package depends on the transaction.

This is one of those moments when buyers sometimes think:

> “I signed everything. Are we closed now?”

Usually, signing alone doesn’t mean the transaction is finished.

There are still steps after the signatures.

That’s why it helps to think of signing as one part of the final sequence, not the finish line itself.

## Step 3: Any Required Funds Need to Be Ready

If the buyer needs to bring funds into escrow for closing, those funds must be received in an acceptable form and available in time for the transaction to move forward.

California guidance specifically warns that closing can be delayed when deposited funds are not considered “good funds” or have not cleared in time.

This is one area where guessing is a bad idea.

If you need to send funds, confirm the amount, timing and approved method directly with your escrow officer.

And because real estate wire fraud exists, don’t rely on unexpected emails or last-minute changes to wiring instructions without independently verifying them through a trusted contact method.

## Step 4: The Lender Funds the Loan

Signing and funding are not the same thing.

**Funding** is when the lender actually provides the loan money for the transaction.

According to the California Department of Real Estate, loan funding occurs after the lender’s conditions have been satisfied and escrow has requested funding. Escrow also needs to confirm that the required conditions on its side of the transaction have been met.

This is why hearing “clear to close” doesn’t mean the money has already moved.

The lender may have approved the loan, but the transaction still has to progress through its final closing steps.

## Step 5: Recording Takes Place

This is one of the most important parts of the process to understand, especially in California.

**Recording** happens when the documents that need to become part of the public record are submitted and recorded with the county where the property is located.

California’s Department of Real Estate explains that the escrow officer authorizes title to record after the conditions of escrow have been satisfied.

Funding and recording also do not necessarily happen at the same moment.

In some transactions, recording may occur the following business day after funding. Some California counties may allow same-day recording depending on timing and circumstances.

So if your loan funded at 3:00 p.m., don’t automatically assume that means you closed at 3:01.

Wait for confirmation.

Read the [California DRE escrow guide](https://www.dre.ca.gov/files/pdf/escrow_info_consumers.pdf) for general consumer information.

## So When Do You Actually Get the Keys?

This is the question buyers really care about.

And the answer depends on the transaction.

Signing documents doesn’t automatically mean you get the keys.

Funding doesn’t always mean you immediately get the keys either.

The transaction still needs to reach the point where the required closing conditions have been completed and recording has been confirmed.

Your real estate agent and escrow team can tell you when the transaction has officially closed and when possession can be delivered according to the purchase agreement.

Until then, keep your phone nearby.

Those final hours can involve communication between the lender, escrow, title, agents and other parties involved in getting the transaction across the finish line.

## Can Something Still Delay Closing After Clear to Close?

Yes.

Clear to close significantly moves the loan forward, but it doesn’t remove every possible issue from the transaction.

A missing signature, funds that haven’t arrived, a final documentation issue or something else that still needs to be resolved can affect timing.

That doesn’t mean buyers should panic after receiving a clear-to-close notice.

It means the file is **close, not complete**.

## What Should You Do After You’re Clear to Close?

You don’t need to chase every person involved in the transaction.

But you should stay available.

Respond when your lender, agent or escrow team asks for something.

Review documents before signing them.

Confirm how and when any required funds need to be delivered.

And if you don’t understand what happens next, ask.

One simple question can clear up a lot of confusion:

> “What are we waiting for before this transaction can close?”

That’s a much better question than assuming everything is finished because the loan is clear to close.

## Clear to Close in California? Know What Comes Next.

Getting the clear-to-close message is worth celebrating.

Just don’t confuse it with the final confirmation.

There are still documents, funds and recording steps that may need to happen before a California escrow is complete.
